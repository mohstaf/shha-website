Von Willebrand disease and rare factor deficiencies

The most common inherited bleeding disorder. It affects men and women equally, and many people have it for years without knowing.

What is it?

The blood does not clot properly because a protein called von Willebrand factor (VWF) is low or does not work well. This protein helps form a plug where a blood vessel is injured.

Most people have a mild form and only notice after an injury, dental work, surgery or childbirth. A few have a severe form.

How is it inherited?

The gene is not on the X chromosome, so boys and girls inherit it equally. In most families, one parent with the changed gene is enough: each child has a 1 in 2 chance of inheriting it.

The severe form usually happens when a child inherits the changed gene from both parents. Bleeding can differ a lot within one family. Read about carrier testing.

Warning signs

  • Frequent or long nosebleeds
  • Bleeding gums and easy bruising
  • Heavy or long periods, often from the first period
  • Heavy bleeding after childbirth, surgery or a tooth extraction

Heavy periods are not always normal. If they last more than a week or cause tiredness, ask about a bleeding test.

In an emergency, such as heavy bleeding or a severe pain crisis, go to the nearest hospital immediately.

Getting tested

A health worker will usually ask for a complete blood count (CBC), a clotting screen, VWF antigen, VWF activity and a factor VIII level.

A normal clotting screen does not rule it out, and levels can change with stress or pregnancy, so tests may need repeating. SHHA can tell you where to go: see the testing centres.

Treatment and living well

Many people need treatment only for injuries, dental work, surgery or childbirth. A doctor chooses it:

  • Tranexamic acid helps clots hold, for nosebleeds, dental work and heavy periods.
  • Desmopressin releases the body's stored VWF. It does not suit every type, so a doctor checks it first.
  • VWF-containing concentrates are for severe bleeding or major surgery, given by a clinician.

Tell every doctor, dentist and midwife about the condition. Avoid aspirin and ibuprofen; paracetamol is safer for pain. Plan childbirth with a doctor.

Rare factor deficiencies

Other clotting factors, such as factor VII, X, XIII or fibrinogen, can also be low. Most are inherited from both parents, so they are seen more often when parents are related.

Signs are like other bleeding disorders; in some types a newborn bleeds from the cord stump. Each needs its own factor test, and factor XIII deficiency does not show on a usual clotting screen.
