Thalassaemia

An inherited condition in which the body makes too little normal haemoglobin. Children with severe forms can grow up well with regular, safe care.

What is it?

Thalassaemia is an inherited condition in which the body makes too little normal haemoglobin, the part of red blood cells that carries oxygen.

Mild forms may cause no symptoms. Severe forms appear in early childhood and need regular blood transfusions.

How is it inherited?

A child has a severe form when both parents carry a thalassaemia gene. Carriers have thalassaemia trait, also called thalassaemia minor. They are usually healthy.

Warning signs

In severe forms, signs usually start in the first two years of life:

  • Pale skin and tiredness
  • Poor growth and feeding
  • A swollen belly
  • Changes in the bones of the face

Getting tested

A health worker will usually ask for a complete blood count (CBC) with red cell indices, then haemoglobin electrophoresis or HPLC.

Thalassaemia trait can look like iron deficiency on a blood count. Do not take iron unless a doctor confirms iron deficiency. See the testing centres.

Treatment and living well

  • Regular, safe blood transfusions for severe forms, with blood that has been screened.
  • Iron chelation, a medicine that removes the extra iron transfusions leave in the body.
  • Folic acid if a doctor prescribes it.
  • Regular check-ups of growth, iron levels, heart and liver.

People with thalassaemia trait usually need no treatment.
