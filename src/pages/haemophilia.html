Haemophilia

An inherited condition in which the blood does not clot properly. With early diagnosis and the right care, people with haemophilia can go to school, work and live full lives.

What is it?

Haemophilia is an inherited condition in which the blood does not clot properly because a clotting factor, factor VIII or factor IX, is missing or low. A small cut is usually not dangerous, but bleeding can go on for a long time, and bleeding inside the body, into joints, muscles or the head, can cause lasting damage.

It mainly affects boys and men. Women and girls can carry the gene, and some carriers have bleeding symptoms too.

There are two main types. Haemophilia A means low factor VIII and is the most common. Haemophilia B means low factor IX. Both can be mild, moderate or severe.

How is it inherited?

The genes for factor VIII and factor IX sit on the X chromosome. Boys have one X chromosome, so one changed copy is enough to cause haemophilia. Girls have two, so the usual copy on the other X normally protects them.

About a third of people with haemophilia have no family history, because the gene can change for the first time in a family. A blood test can show whether a woman is a carrier. Read about carrier testing.

Warning signs

In Somalia, many boys are first noticed because of heavy bleeding after circumcision. See a health worker if a child has:

  • Bleeding that will not stop after circumcision, a cut, a tooth extraction or an injection
  • Large bruises from small bumps
  • Swollen, painful joints or muscles, often the knees, elbows or ankles
  • Bleeding from the gums or nose that is hard to stop
  • Blood in the urine or stool

Women and girls who carry the gene may have heavy periods or heavy bleeding after childbirth.

Getting tested

Diagnosis starts with a blood test. A health worker will usually ask for:

  • A complete blood count (CBC)
  • A clotting screen, including the activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT)
  • A factor VIII or factor IX assay, which confirms the type and how severe it is

Not every laboratory in Somalia can do a factor assay. SHHA can tell you where to go. See testing centres, or message us on WhatsApp.

Treatment and living well

The main treatment is clotting factor concentrate, given into a vein to replace the missing factor. It is not yet available in the Somali public health system, and SHHA is working with partners to bring in donated treatment for use by qualified clinicians.

What families can do

  • Tell every doctor, dentist and nurse that your child has haemophilia, before any procedure, including circumcision.
  • For a bleed into a joint or muscle: rest, a cold pack wrapped in cloth, gentle pressure and raising the limb, then seek care.
  • Do not give aspirin or ibuprofen. They make bleeding worse. Paracetamol is safer for pain.
  • Avoid injections into the muscle. Vaccines can be given under the skin with pressure afterwards.
  • Keep active with safer sports such as swimming and walking. Strong muscles protect the joints.
  • Look after teeth and gums to avoid dental bleeding.
